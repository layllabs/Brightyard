A storm guide for Valley homeowners

Getting your yard ready for heavy rain

Valley yards spend most of the year dry, so a big storm finds every clogged drain, bare slope, and low spot at once. Here's what to do before, during, and after heavy rain to protect your plants, your slopes, and your house.

By Joaquin, owner of Brightyard · Last reviewed October 23, 2026

The short version

  • Turn off your sprinklers before the rain starts and leave them off until the soil dries out again.
  • Clear every path water takes: gutters, downspouts, yard drains, and the curb in front of your house.
  • Point water away from the house. Downspouts should empty several feet from the foundation, never against it.
  • Cover bare slopes and use sandbags to steer water, not to dam it.
  • Check your trees. Soaked soil plus wind is when trees and big limbs come down.
  • Stay off soaked beds and lawns afterward. Walking and digging on wet clay compacts it for months.

One to two days out

Before the storm

  1. Turn off the irrigation. Switch the controller to off or "rain delay." A smart controller with a rain sensor may do this itself, but check.
  2. Clear gutters and downspouts. The first big storm of the season carries a year's worth of leaves, needles, and dust into them.
  3. Extend downspouts so they empty several feet away from the foundation, onto a splash block, extension, or a planted area that can take the water.
  4. Clear yard drains and catch basins. Pull the grates, scoop out leaves and silt, and run a hose through to make sure water moves.
  5. Bag leaves and clippings. Put them in the green bin. Don't blow or hose them into the street. Anything that reaches a storm drain can clog it mid-storm, and it flows untreated to the ocean.
  6. Secure loose items. Patio umbrellas, cushions, trash cans, and lightweight pots can blow around in storm winds.
  7. Help containers drain. Make sure pots have drainage holes, lift them off their saucers, and move rain-sensitive plants like succulents under an eave.
  8. Cover bare soil in flat beds with wood-chip mulch to cut down on splash and erosion.
Hillside homes

Slopes and sandbags

Bare or freshly cleared slopes are where most yard storm damage starts. Water that runs across bare soil picks up speed and carries the soil with it.

  • Cover bare slopes with heavy plastic sheeting, overlapped like roof shingles from the top down and weighted with sandbags. Straw wattles laid across the slope also slow the water down.
  • Use sandbags to steer water, not stop it. Lay them to guide runoff toward a drain, the street, or a safe outlet, never so that water pools against a wall or your house.
  • Keep drainage channels open. Clear the V-ditches and terrace drains on hillside lots, since they're designed to carry water off the slope.
  • Watch for warning signs: new cracks in the soil, bulging at the bottom of a slope, or muddy water seeping out of a hillside. Move away from the slope, and call 911 if it threatens a home or road.
Free sandbags

In the City of Los Angeles, every neighborhood fire station has free, ready-to-fill sandbags year-round, up to 25 per household. Free sand is available at select stations during storm season, so call ahead, or fill the bags with soil from your yard. Outside the city, Los Angeles County Fire and Ventura County Fire post their own sandbag locations online.

If your home is near a recent burn area, such as the Kenneth Fire area around West Hills, Calabasas, and Hidden Hills or the Palisades Fire area in the Santa Monica Mountains, heavy rain can trigger mud and debris flows. Sign up for emergency alerts (NotifyLA in the City of Los Angeles, Alert LA County, or VC Alert in Ventura County) and leave when officials tell you to.

Wind plus wet soil

Trees and wind

Saturated soil loosens its grip on roots, and that's when wind brings down trees and large limbs. Before the storm, walk around your trees and look for:

  • Dead or hanging branches, especially over the house, driveway, or power lines.
  • A new or worsening lean, or soil cracking or lifting on one side of the trunk.
  • Cracks, splits, or cavities in the trunk or where major limbs join it.
  • Mushrooms or soft, crumbly wood at the base.

If you see any of these on a large tree, have an ISA-certified arborist look at it. Never prune near power lines yourself. In the City of Los Angeles, report a fallen or dangerous street tree by calling 311. If one does come down, see what to do if a city tree falls.

While it's raining

During the storm

  • Watch where the water goes. Take a short video of puddles, overflowing gutters, and water heading toward the house. It's the best information you'll have for fixing drainage later.
  • Stay off slopes and away from fast-moving water in channels and streets.
  • Stay away from downed power lines and anything touching them. Keep at least 100 feet away and call 911.
  • Follow evacuation orders if you live near a recent burn area. Mud and debris flows can happen with little warning.
The days that follow

After the storm

Leave the sprinklers off

After a soaking rain, Valley clay soil can stay wet for a week or more. Use the screwdriver test from our plant care guide: if a screwdriver slides easily 4 to 6 inches into the soil, there's no need to water. Restart irrigation zone by zone as each area dries out.

Look for standing water

Puddles that last more than a day or two point to a drainage problem. Roots sitting in waterlogged clay run out of air and rot, so watch nearby plants for yellowing or wilting in the following weeks. Empty saucers, buckets, and anything else holding water so it doesn't become a mosquito nursery.

Check the trees again

Look for a new lean, lifted soil around the base, or cracked limbs. Trees sometimes fail a day or two after a storm, once the wind picks up on still-wet soil.

Stay off wet beds

Walking, digging, or running equipment on soaked clay compacts it, and compacted clay sheds water for years. Wait until the soil crumbles in your hand instead of smearing.

Clean up and re-mulch

Rake washed-out mulch back into place, clear silt from drains and walkways, and top up any bare spots. Bag storm debris for the green bin.

Get ahead of the weeds

Rain sets off a flush of weed seedlings within two to three weeks. Hoe or pull them while they're small and the soil is soft, before they set seed.

Hold off on fertilizer

Rain doesn't create an emergency need to feed. Most established plants don't need fertilizer in fall, and citrus feeding waits until January or February.

Plant once it drains

Fall rain is the start of the best planting season in Southern California. Once the soil has drained enough to work, it's a great time to put in new shrubs, trees, and California natives.

Fix it before next time

Drainage problems worth fixing

A storm shows you exactly where your yard's drainage fails. Note these and plan fixes for the dry months:

  • Water flowing toward the house. Soil and hardscape next to the foundation should slope away from it.
  • Downspouts dumping at the foundation. Extend them, or route them into a planted area that can soak water up.
  • Low spots that hold water for days. These usually need regrading, a drain, or plants that tolerate wet feet.
  • Drains that overflowed. They may be clogged further down the line, or simply too small for the area they serve.
  • Gullies on slopes. Bare spots that eroded need planting, mulch, or erosion netting before the next storm.

Some fixes can also earn a rebate. Rain gardens and other rain-capture features count toward LADWP's Turf Replacement Rebate when you replace a lawn.

Common questions

Questions about rain and your yard

Should I turn off my sprinklers when it rains?

Yes. Turn them off before the storm and leave them off until the soil dries out. Watering soaked ground wastes water and causes runoff, which LADWP's rules prohibit.

How long should I wait to water after heavy rain?

Until the soil has dried below the surface, which can take a week or more in clay after a big storm. Push a long screwdriver into the soil. If it slides in easily 4 to 6 inches, there's still enough water down there.

Where can I get free sandbags in the San Fernando Valley?

In the City of Los Angeles, every LAFD neighborhood fire station has free, ready-to-fill sandbags year-round, up to 25 per household, with free sand at select stations during storm season. Call ahead to check supply. Outside the city, check Los Angeles County Fire's or Ventura County Fire's sandbag locations.

Should I put plastic on my slope before it rains?

On bare or freshly cleared slopes, yes. Use heavy plastic sheeting overlapped from the top down like roof shingles and weighted with sandbags, so water runs off the plastic instead of carrying soil away. Planted slopes with healthy groundcover usually don't need it.

Can heavy rain kill my plants?

It can. Valley clay drains slowly, and roots that sit in waterlogged soil for days run out of air and rot. California natives and succulents are the most sensitive. Good drainage and leaving the sprinklers off afterward are the best protection.

Why does my yard flood or hold puddles after rain?

Usually it's a combination of slow-draining clay, low spots, compacted soil, and downspouts or drains that empty in the wrong place. Watch where water collects during the storm, then fix the grade, extend the downspouts, or add drainage during the dry season.

Is it safe to work in the yard right after a storm?

Wait. Stay away from downed lines, damaged trees, and unstable slopes, and keep off soaked beds and lawns until the soil firms up. Working wet clay compacts it and damages roots.

Should I fertilize after it rains?

No need. Most established plants don't need fertilizer in fall. Hold citrus feeding until January or February, and let lawns and beds dry out first.
