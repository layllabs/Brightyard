A removal guide for Valley homeowners

Removing a well-established hibiscus

A hibiscus that's been in the ground for years has a woody trunk, a wide root ball, and a habit of growing back from whatever you leave behind. Cutting it to the ground is the easy half. Here's how to get the roots out so it stays gone, and what to check before you start.

By Joaquin, owner of Brightyard · Last reviewed November 16, 2026

The short version

  • Cutting it down isn't the same as removing it. Hibiscus regrows from low stubs and live roots, so plan on digging out the root ball.
  • Dig when the soil is moist. UC's pest management program says digging in dry, hard soil breaks stems off and leaves roots behind to resprout. After a rain, or a slow soak the day before, is the time.
  • Call 811 two working days before you dig. It's free, and utilities ask for it before any digging, removing a stump included.
  • Leave a 3- to 4-foot stump when you cut the plant down. You'll use it as a lever to rock the root ball loose.
  • Look for nests first. State law protects active bird nests, and a dense hibiscus is a likely spot for one from late winter through summer.
  • Watch the spot for a year. Pull any sprouts as soon as they show, roots and all.

Check first

Before you start

Make sure it's yours to remove

If the hibiscus grows in the parkway, the strip between the sidewalk and the curb, check with the city before you touch it. In the City of Los Angeles, street trees in the parkway usually belong to the city, and pruning or removing one needs a StreetsLA permit. Our guide to what to do if a city tree falls has the StreetsLA contacts. If you live in an HOA, check whether the plant sits in a common area.

On your own property, hibiscus isn't one of the native trees and shrubs on the City of Los Angeles protected list. Our plant care guide covers which ones are.

Call 811

Request a free utility locate by calling 811 or going to DigAlert, the 811 center for Southern California, at least two working days before you dig. Utilities ask for this before any digging project, removing a stump included. Underground utility lines are marked for free.

811 doesn't mark your sprinklers

Irrigation pipe and drip tubing often run right through the bed around an old shrub. Run the zone for a minute before you dig to see where the heads are, then dig carefully near them. If you're not sure what's down there, our guide to sprinkler systems explains what you're likely to find.

Look for nests

A mature hibiscus is dense and leafy, the kind of shrub birds build in. California Fish and Game Code section 3503 makes it unlawful to needlessly destroy the nest or eggs of any bird. Ventura County's guidance treats January 1 through September 15 as nesting season and says any nest you find should be avoided. Before you cut, look into the canopy for a few minutes. If adults are flying in and out or sitting on a nest, wait until it's empty.

Timing

When to do it

UC's guide to removing woody shrubs says to dig in early spring or late fall, when the soil is moist and the roots come out easily. Digging in dry, hard soil usually snaps the stems off and leaves the roots in the ground to resprout.

In the Valley, that points to late fall and winter, after the first good rain. It also keeps you mostly outside nesting season. If it hasn't rained, UC notes that irrigating ahead of time can loosen the soil: give the area a slow, deep soak the day before you dig. Moist clay digs far more easily than dry clay, and the roots come out in bigger pieces.

What you'll need

Tools for the job

  • Gloves, eye protection, and closed shoes.
  • Loppers and a pruning saw for the branches and trunk.
  • A sharp spade or trenching shovel for the trench around the root ball.
  • A mattock or digging bar for clay and for prying. A UC Master Gardener column calls the mattock a heavy hitter for grubbing out unwanted plants.
  • A root saw or reciprocating saw with a pruning blade for roots too thick for loppers.
  • A tarp to pile branches and soil on, so the cleanup goes faster.
How to do it

Step by step

  1. Take the canopy down. Work from the outside in with loppers and a saw until you're down to the main trunk or trunks. Cut those to about 3 to 4 feet tall, not flush with the ground. The stump is your lever for the rest of the job.
  2. Find the irrigation and wet the soil. Mark any sprinkler heads or drip lines near the plant. If the ground is dry, soak it slowly the day before.
  3. Dig a trench around the root ball. Start about a foot out from the trunk for a medium plant, farther for an old one, and work your way around. Cut roots as you hit them.
  4. Undercut it. Once the trench goes all the way around, angle the spade under the root ball and cut the roots running down and underneath.
  5. Rock it loose. Push and pull the stump back and forth. Each time it moves, look for the root that's still holding and cut it. When the root ball moves freely, lift or roll it out of the hole. A big one is heavy, so knock the soil off or cut it into pieces rather than straining your back.
  6. Clear the hole. Pull out the thick roots left in the bottom and sides of the hole. Root pieces left in the ground are what grow back.
  7. Backfill and settle. Put the soil back, tamp it lightly, and water it in.
So it stays gone

Stopping regrowth

Tropical hibiscus handles hard pruning well. Britain's Royal Horticultural Society notes that strong growth can be cut back to a few inches from the base and it will regrow. That's useful when you want to keep the plant. When you don't, it means a stump left in the ground is likely to send up new shoots.

UC's guidance on woody shrubs is the same: many of them resprout from the root crown after they're cut, and cutting alone won't get rid of them unless you keep doing it. Digging out the root crown is the reliable fix.

After removal

Check the spot every week or two. If a shoot comes up, dig it out with whatever root it's attached to instead of snapping it off at the surface. UC says to watch a removal area closely for at least a year.

If you can't dig it out

Sometimes the root ball is tight against a foundation, wall, or patio. You have two options:

  • Cut it low and keep cutting. Remove every sprout as it appears. It's slow, but each round uses up what's stored in the roots.
  • Cut it low and treat the fresh cut. UC describes painting or squirting a cut-stump herbicide, such as a triclopyr or glyphosate product, onto the cut immediately after cutting, because waiting gives poor results. Use only a product labeled for cut stumps, and follow the label exactly.
Disposal

Cleanup and disposal

  • Branches and leaves go in the green bin in the City of LA. Cut them down so they fit inside the bin.
  • Soil stays home. LA County's green cart guidance lists dirt and rocks as not accepted, so knock the soil off the root ball and put it back in the hole.
  • Overflow: in the City of LA, LA Sanitation's free annual brush pickup takes yard waste that won't fit in the bin. Ask about root balls and stumps when you schedule it.

Rules differ in Calabasas, Thousand Oaks, and unincorporated LA County. Our green bin guide covers each one.

FAQ

Common questions

Will a hibiscus grow back if I cut it to the ground?

Usually, yes. Tropical hibiscus regrows from low on its stems, and UC says many woody shrubs resprout from the root crown after they're cut. Cutting alone only works if you keep cutting the regrowth. To be done with it, dig out the root ball.

When is the best time to remove a hibiscus?

When the soil is moist. UC's guidance for removing woody shrubs is early spring or late fall. In the Valley, late fall and winter after a good rain is ideal, and it's mostly outside bird nesting season. If it hasn't rained, soak the area slowly the day before.

Do I need to call 811 to dig out a shrub?

Call 811 or request a ticket through DigAlert at least two working days before you dig. It's free, and utilities ask for it before any digging, removing a stump included. 811 doesn't mark private sprinkler lines, so find those yourself.

Can I remove a hibiscus during bird nesting season?

Check it carefully first. California Fish and Game Code section 3503 makes it unlawful to needlessly destroy the nest or eggs of any bird, and Ventura County's guidance treats January 1 through September 15 as nesting season. If you find an active nest, wait until it's empty.

Do I need a permit to remove a hibiscus?

On your own property, hibiscus isn't on the City of Los Angeles protected tree and shrub list. If it grows in the parkway between the sidewalk and the curb, check with StreetsLA first, because street trees there usually belong to the city and need a permit to remove. HOAs may have their own rules.

What if I can't dig the stump out?

Cut it low and either keep removing every sprout as it appears, or treat the fresh cut right away with a cut-stump herbicide labeled for that use, following the label exactly. UC says to apply it immediately after cutting and to watch the area for at least a year.

Can hibiscus branches go in the green bin?

Yes. In the City of LA, branches and leaves go in the green bin, cut down so they fit inside. Knock the soil off the roots first, because dirt isn't accepted in green carts. LA Sanitation's free annual brush pickup takes the overflow.
