A local guide for homeowners

Everyday plant care for San Fernando Valley yards

Most plant advice online is written for places with summer rain and mild heat. The Valley has neither. This guide covers what actually keeps yards healthy here, from Granada Hills to Thousand Oaks, whether you do the work yourself or have someone do it for you.

By Joaquin, owner of Brightyard · Last reviewed October 20, 2026

The short version

  • Water deep and less often. Short daily sprinkles grow shallow roots that fry in July.
  • Check the soil, not the calendar. A screwdriver pushed into the bed tells you more than the timer does.
  • Mulch every bare spot you can, 3 to 4 inches deep, kept a hand's width off trunks and stems.
  • Prune for the plant, not for neatness, and at the right time of year for that plant.
  • Plant in fall. October through March gives roots a cool, rainy season to settle in before the heat.
  • Look before you spray. Most "pest" problems trace back to watering, ants, or the wrong plant in the wrong spot.

The starting point

Why Valley yards are different

The San Fernando Valley is an inland basin. Summers are long, dry, and hot: Woodland Hills reached 121°F in September 2020, the highest temperature ever recorded in Los Angeles County. Nearly all of the year's rain falls between November and March, and on the coldest winter nights the valley floor can drop close to freezing, especially in low spots in the west Valley. Much of the Valley also sits on heavy, clay-rich soil that soaks up water slowly and holds it for a long time.

That adds up to three things worth keeping in mind:

  • Summer is the stress season, not winter. Most plant losses here happen between July and September.
  • Watering is the biggest lever you control, and the one most often set wrong.
  • Plants from similar climates do best with the least help: California natives and plants from the Mediterranean, South Africa, and parts of Australia.
The part that matters most

Watering: deep and infrequent beats short and daily

Roots grow where the water is. Short, frequent watering keeps moisture in the top inch or two of soil, so roots stay shallow and cook when the heat arrives. Longer, less frequent watering pushes moisture, and roots, down to where the soil stays cool.

How to tell if a bed needs water

Push a long screwdriver or a thin piece of rebar into the soil a few inches from the plant. If it slides in easily 4 to 6 inches, there's still water down there. If it stops hard near the surface, the soil is dry or badly compacted. Do this before you add another day or more minutes to the timer.

Clay soil: cycle and soak

Clay takes water in slowly. If you see water running off a bed or lawn before it soaks in, split the run time into two shorter cycles with a break of 30 minutes or more in between. You get the same amount of water into the ground with far less of it ending up in the gutter.

If you're an LADWP customer
  • Watering days: street addresses ending in an odd number water Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Even numbers water Sunday, Tuesday, and Thursday.
  • No sprinkler watering between 9 a.m. and 4 p.m.
  • Standard spray nozzles: one cycle of up to 8 minutes per station per watering day.
  • Water-conserving nozzles: two cycles of up to 15 minutes each per station per watering day.
  • No runoff onto sidewalks, driveways, or gutters.
  • Hand watering with a shut-off nozzle is allowed any day before 9 a.m. or after 4 p.m.

The City of Los Angeles is in Phase 2 of its water conservation ordinance as of this update. Cities outside LADWP's service area, including much of the Calabasas–Agoura Hills–Westlake Village area and the Conejo Valley, are served by other water providers with their own schedules, so check with yours.

One practical upgrade: older spray nozzles are a big reason Valley lawns look stressed under the 8-minute limit. Water-conserving rotary nozzles put water down more slowly, which suits clay, and they qualify for the longer two-cycle allowance.

Drip for beds, shrubs, and trees

Anything that isn't lawn does better on drip than on overhead spray. Drip puts water at the roots, loses very little to wind and evaporation, and keeps leaves dry, which cuts down on fungal problems.

These are starting points, not rules. Clay holds water longer than sandy soil, and shade, slope, and wind all change the picture. Let the screwdriver test have the final say. New plantings need more frequent water for their first one or two summers.

The whole-yard foundation

Soil and mulch

Healthy soil does most of the work that fertilizer gets credit for. Take care of the ground and the plants mostly take care of themselves.

  • Mulch bare soil 3 to 4 inches deep with wood chips. It keeps roots cool, holds moisture, smothers weed seedlings, and feeds the soil as it breaks down. Keep it a hand's width back from trunks and stems, because mulch piled against bark invites rot.
  • Go easy on rock and gravel in hot spots. Rock soaks up afternoon sun and radiates it back onto plants and walls. Wood mulch is kinder to plants in full-sun beds near the house.
  • Don't overload planting holes with amendments. For trees and shrubs, backfill with the soil you dug out. A hole full of rich mix inside clay acts like a bathtub and holds water around the roots. Improve clay from the top down with mulch instead. Vegetable beds are the exception: they do benefit from compost worked in.
  • Leave grass clippings on the lawn. They break down quickly and return nitrogen to the soil.
  • Fertilize less than you think. Most established trees and shrubs in the Valley need little or no fertilizer. Lawns, citrus, and vegetables are the main exceptions.
  • Stay off wet clay. Digging or walking on soaked clay compacts it, and compacted clay sheds water for years.
Cut with a reason

Pruning with a purpose

The most common pruning mistake in Valley yards is shearing every shrub into a ball or a box on every visit. It looks tidy for a week, but it cuts off next season's flower buds, leaves a thin shell of leaves over a bare woody interior, and keeps the plant spending its energy on regrowth.

A better approach: remove dead, damaged, and crossing branches first. Then thin by cutting a branch back to where it meets another branch, instead of trimming the tips of everything. As a general rule, don't take off more than about a quarter of a plant's leafy growth in a single year.

Diagnose first

Pests: look before you spray

The University of California recommends an approach called integrated pest management: figure out what's actually happening, fix the cause, and reach for the least-toxic option only when it's needed.

  1. Identify it. Turn over a few leaves. Take a clear photo. Many "pests" are actually water stress, sunburn, or a nutrient issue.
  2. Tolerate small numbers. A few aphids feed the ladybugs and lacewings that keep them in check.
  3. Fix the cause. Stressed plants attract pests. Correct the watering, sun, or crowding first.
  4. Use physical controls. A strong spray of water knocks off aphids, whitefly, and spider mites. Hand-pick snails at night.
  5. Treat last, and narrowly. If you do treat, choose a targeted product and avoid spraying open flowers, which harms bees and the beneficial insects you want to keep.

Common Valley culprits

  • Ants are often the real problem. They farm aphids, scale, and mealybugs for their honeydew and guard them from predators. Control the ants with bait stations, plus a sticky barrier on a trunk wrapped in tape first, and the natural predators can catch up.
  • Gophers: trapping is the most effective option. Plant new shrubs and trees in wire baskets.
  • Snails and slugs: hand-pick after dark, remove hiding spots like ivy and boards, and trim citrus branches up off the ground.
  • Spider mites and whitefly: worst on dusty, drought-stressed plants in summer. Rinse the undersides of leaves and fix the watering.
The Valley's favorite tree

Citrus care

Yellow leaves

  • In winter: cold soil slows how much nutrition roots can take up, and leaves often yellow. They usually green up as the soil warms in spring. Don't respond with a big dose of fertilizer in December.
  • Older leaves yellowing evenly: usually a nitrogen shortage.
  • Yellow between green veins on new leaves: usually a micronutrient issue such as zinc or iron. UC recommends a chelated micronutrient spray on the leaves as new growth emerges in spring.
  • Yellowing plus leaf drop in clay: often too much water. Let the top few inches dry out between deep waterings.

Feeding

UC guidance is to apply nitrogen in January or February, just before bloom, with a second feeding in May and possibly a third in June. Avoid feeding late in the season, which can hurt fruit quality and delay coloring.

Watering

Deep and infrequent. Keep sprinklers and drip emitters off the trunk itself. Some fruit drop in late spring and early summer is normal: the tree is shedding fruit it can't carry.

Citrus quarantine

Southern California is under quarantine for the Asian citrus psyllid, the insect that spreads huanglongbing (HLB, also called citrus greening), a tree disease with no cure. Don't move citrus plants, leaves, or stems out of your area. If you share backyard fruit, remove the stems and leaves and wash it first. Report a suspected case to the California Department of Food and Agriculture at 800-491-1899.

The thirstiest part of the yard

Lawns: keep, shrink, or replace

Dead or dormant?

Warm-season grasses like Bermuda, St. Augustine, and kikuyu turn tan in winter and green up in spring. Cool-season tall fescue stays green in winter but struggles in summer heat. For a quick check, grab a handful and tug. If it lifts out with no resistance and the roots are brown and brittle, that patch is dead. If it holds, it's dormant or stressed and can recover.

Brown patches in summer

These are usually a sprinkler coverage problem, not a fertilizer problem. Set a few empty tuna cans around the lawn, run one cycle, and compare how much water each can catches. The dry patches are almost always where the cans come up short. Fix heads that are tilted, blocked, misting, or broken before changing the schedule.

Mowing

Keep tall fescue on the high side in summer, around 3 inches. Never remove more than a third of the blade in one mowing, and leave the clippings.

Replacing a lawn

LADWP's Turf Replacement Rebate pays residential customers $5 per square foot, up to 5,000 square feet, for a maximum of $25,000. You need pre-approval before you touch the grass. The finished project needs California Friendly plants, mulch, drip irrigation, and a rain-capture feature such as a rain garden or rain barrel, and it has to stay compliant for five years. Other water providers in the Valley and Ventura County run similar programs through SoCal Water$mart at their own rates. Program amounts change, so confirm the current terms before starting.

There's also a middle path: shrink the lawn to the area you actually use, and turn the rest into mulched planting beds on drip.

Extreme days

Heat waves and Santa Ana winds

Before a heat wave

  • Water deeply a day or two ahead so the root zone starts full.
  • Top up mulch on bare spots.
  • Move containers out of afternoon sun and group them together.
  • Hold off on pruning and fertilizing. Both push tender new growth that scorches.

During a heat wave

  • Afternoon wilting that recovers by evening is normal. It's how many plants cope with heat. Check the soil before adding water. Overwatering clay during a heat wave is how roots rot.
  • Containers and new plantings may need water daily.
  • Skip the light daily sprinkle. It mostly evaporates and keeps roots shallow.
  • Shade young plants with shade cloth. Young fruit and citrus trunks can be protected from sunburn with white interior latex paint thinned with water.

Santa Ana winds

These dry winds usually arrive between fall and early winter and pull moisture out of leaves and soil fast. Check soil moisture after a wind event even if it isn't hot, stake or secure young trees, and clear dead palm fronds, leaves, and debris from roofs and gutters, especially on hillside properties.

For hillside homes

Hillside and fire-season upkeep

Many of the Valley's hillside neighborhoods fall in a Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone. Within the City of Los Angeles, the fire department treats brush clearance as a year-round responsibility. The main requirements:

  • Cut grass and reduce native brush to about 3 inches within 200 feet of structures, with exceptions for well-spaced individual native shrubs.
  • Keep 10 feet clear along combustible fences and driveways, and 10 feet between foliage and any chimney outlet.
  • On trees taller than 18 feet, remove lower branches so no foliage is within 6 feet of the ground.
  • Keep 5 feet of clearance between your roof and any overhanging branches, and keep roofs and gutters free of leaves and debris.
  • Don't clear brush on Red Flag days, and use non-sparking blades.

You can look up your parcel's requirements on the LAFD's Fire Inspection Management System (FIMS). Outside the city, Los Angeles County and Ventura County fire departments set their own rules. California's new "Zone 0" rules for the first five feet around a home were approved by the state Board of Forestry in August 2026 but, as of this update, are not yet in effect. We'll update this page when they are.

Know before you cut

Protected trees

The City of Los Angeles protects most native oaks (scrub oak is the exception), western sycamore, Southern California black walnut, and California bay, along with two native shrubs, toyon and Mexican elderberry, once they measure at least 4 inches in diameter at 4½ feet above the ground. Removing or relocating one requires a city permit, and the ordinance also prohibits damaging them. Thousand Oaks, Calabasas, and other nearby cities have their own oak rules.

For established native oaks, keep sprinklers and regular irrigation away from the trunk and root crown. They evolved with dry summers, and summer water around the trunk can lead to root disease. For pruning, hire an ISA-certified arborist.

Everyday care

The 10-minute weekly yard walk

Most problems are easy to fix when they're caught early. Once a week, take ten minutes and walk the yard with a screwdriver and your phone.

  1. Test the soil in two or three beds with the screwdriver. Moist at 4 to 6 inches is the goal.
  2. Look at the newest growth first. Wilting, yellowing, and pests usually show up there before anywhere else.
  3. Flip a few leaves and check the undersides for aphids, whitefly, or fine webbing from mites.
  4. Watch one irrigation cycle a month for broken heads, misting, runoff, and dry spots.
  5. Pull weeds while they're small, before they set seed.
  6. Clear debris from drains, walkways, and the base of plants.
  7. Take a photo of anything that looks off, so you can compare next week.
Common questions

Questions Valley homeowners ask most

How often should I water my yard in the San Fernando Valley?

Less often than most people think, but more deeply. LADWP customers can water three days a week based on their address, outside the 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. window. Use those days for longer, deeper runs, split into two cycles on clay soil, and check the soil with a screwdriver before adding more. Established shrubs on drip often need water only every 3 to 7 days in summer, and mature trees every 2 to 4 weeks.

Why are my plants wilting in the afternoon even though I water every day?

Afternoon wilting that recovers by evening is a normal response to heat, not a sign of thirst. If plants stay wilted into the morning and the soil is wet, the problem may be too much water: roots sitting in soggy clay rot and can't take up water. Check the soil before adding more.

Is my lawn dead or just dormant?

Grab a handful and tug. If it lifts out with no resistance and the roots are brown and brittle, it's dead. If it holds, it's dormant or stressed. Warm-season grasses like Bermuda and St. Augustine go tan every winter and green up in spring. Brown patches in summer are usually a sprinkler coverage problem.

Why are my citrus tree's leaves turning yellow?

In winter, cold soil slows nutrient uptake, and the leaves usually green up in spring. Even yellowing on older leaves points to a nitrogen shortage, and yellow between green veins on new leaves points to a micronutrient issue like zinc or iron. Yellowing with leaf drop in clay soil is often overwatering. Feed citrus in January or February, again in May, and possibly in June, not late in the year.

When is the best time to plant in Southern California?

Fall. Planting between October and March lets roots establish during the cool, rainy months, so plants are ready for their first summer. Spring planting works too, but the plants will need closer watering through the heat.

My gardener shears every shrub into a ball. Is that bad for the plants?

Over time, yes. Constant shearing cuts off flower buds and creates a thin shell of leaves over bare wood, and many shrubs never fill back in. Ask for thinning cuts instead, with each plant pruned at the right time of year, and shearing kept to formal hedges.

Should I replace my lawn with drought-tolerant plants?

If you don't use the lawn, it's usually worth it. LADWP's Turf Replacement Rebate pays residential customers $5 per square foot, up to $25,000, but you must get pre-approval before removing any grass. Many homeowners shrink the lawn to the area they use and convert the rest to mulched beds on drip.

Should I add compost to my clay soil?

For vegetable beds, yes. For trees and shrubs, backfill planting holes with the native soil and spread wood-chip mulch on top instead. Heavily amended holes in clay hold water around the roots like a bathtub, while mulch improves the soil from the top down over time.

Is rock or wood mulch better?

For planting beds, wood mulch. It cools the soil, holds moisture, and feeds soil life as it breaks down. Rock heats up in the sun and radiates that heat onto plants and walls. Use rock for paths, dry creek beds, and areas without plants.

When should I prune roses in the Valley?

From January to mid-February, while the plants are dormant. Remove dead and crossing canes, open up the center, and cut back to healthy, outward-facing buds.

How do I get rid of gophers?

Trapping is the most effective method. Protect new trees and shrubs by planting them in wire baskets, and check for fresh mounds weekly so a new arrival is caught early.

What should a good maintenance gardener do besides mow, blow, and go?

Check and adjust the irrigation with the seasons, fix broken heads and leaks, prune each plant at the right time instead of shearing everything, pull weeds before they seed, keep mulch topped up, and tell you when something looks wrong. A good gardener is watching the health of the yard, not only its edges.
