HOA landscape maintenance in the San Fernando Valley

What a common-area maintenance contract should cover, how California splits the work between the association and homeowners, the state turf rule that reaches HOA common areas on January 1, 2029, and how Brightyard works with associations in the Valley and Ventura County.

By Joaquin, owner of Brightyard · Last reviewed November 19, 2026

The short version

  • Put the scope in writing, area by area. A good contract lists every common area the landscaper covers, what happens on a routine visit, how often, and what counts as extra work.
  • Irrigation is where water and money leak. Ask for regular checks of every zone and seasonal controller changes, not just mowing and blowing.
  • Know who maintains what. Unless your CC&Rs say otherwise, California law makes the association responsible for the common area and each owner responsible for maintaining exclusive use areas like their patio.
  • The turf deadline is January 1, 2029. From then on, HOA common areas can't use drinking water on purely decorative grass. Trees and other plants can still be watered, and nothing has to be torn out.
  • The association can't block water-wise yards. Under Civil Code section 4735, HOA rules can't ban drought-tolerant plants or artificial turf.
  • Gas leaf blowers are banned within 500 feet of a home in the City of Los Angeles, and the rule applies to whoever hired the crew as well as the operator.
  • Ask where the green waste goes. California's organic waste law treats landscape and pruning waste as organic waste to be recycled, not thrown in the trash.

Before you sign

What should an HOA landscape maintenance contract include?

A vague scope is hard to hold anyone to, for the board and the landscaper alike. These are the items worth spelling out:

  • The areas covered. Entries, slopes, pool and clubhouse grounds, parkways, and whether any exclusive use areas are included.
  • The routine visit. Mowing, edging, weeding, trimming, litter pickup in beds, and cleanup of hard surfaces.
  • Frequency, by area and season. Some tasks happen every visit, others monthly or a few times a year.
  • Irrigation. How often every zone gets checked, who adjusts the controllers, which repairs are included, and which need approval.
  • Trees. What height of pruning is included. California's Contractors State License Board (CSLB) lists tree pruning above 15 feet, tree removal, and stump removal as work that needs a licensed contractor.
  • Extras and approvals. How out-of-scope work is quoted and who can approve it, ideally with a dollar limit above which the board signs off.
  • Green waste. Who hauls it and where it goes.
  • Communication. One contact on each side, how residents report problems (usually through management rather than the crew), and what the landscaper reports after visits.
  • Access and hours. Gate codes, pool keys, and the hours work can happen.
  • Term and notice. The start date, how either side can end the agreement, and how much notice that takes.
When it's more than maintenance

Installing a new irrigation system or converting lawn to planting beds is contractor work. CSLB says a project needs a licensed contractor once it reaches $1,000 in combined labor and materials, needs a permit, or involves hired workers. Get those bids from licensed landscape contractors and look up the license on cslb.ca.gov.

Who's responsible

Common area vs. exclusive use common area

Start with your CC&Rs, because they control. When they don't say otherwise, the Davis-Stirling Act sets the default in Civil Code section 4775: the association repairs, replaces, and maintains the common area, and each owner maintains the exclusive use common area attached to their unit. Since 2017, the association has been responsible for repairing and replacing exclusive use common area unless the governing documents say otherwise.

Exclusive use common area is a part of the common area set aside for one unit, such as a patio or balcony. Those are the spots where landscaping contracts tend to get fuzzy, so name them in the contract either way.

Timing

How often should HOA common areas be serviced?

It depends on the property, how residents use it, and the season, so it's best settled on a walkthrough. A few things to plan around:

  • Entries, walkways, and pool areas are what residents and visitors see every day, so they usually get the most frequent visits.
  • Slopes and back areas can often go longer between visits, but they need attention before the rainy season and in fire-prone hillside areas.
  • Growth slows in winter and picks up fast in spring, so one schedule rarely fits the whole year.

Water

Irrigation and watering rules

Broken heads, leaking valves, and controllers left on a summer schedule waste water quietly, and the association pays for it. The only reliable way to find those problems is to run each zone and walk it.

If your community is served by LADWP

  • Sprinklers run on assigned days: odd-numbered addresses on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, and even-numbered addresses on Sunday, Tuesday, and Thursday.
  • No outdoor watering from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m.
  • Standard spray nozzles get one cycle of up to 8 minutes per station per watering day. Water-conserving nozzles get two cycles of up to 15 minutes.
  • No runoff, no watering hard surfaces, and leaks have to be fixed promptly.

Outside LADWP's area, such as the Las Virgenes Municipal Water District cities, check your provider's current rules. Our sprinkler guide covers head types, clay soil, and rebates.

Coming in 2029

The January 1, 2029 turf deadline for HOA common areas

A state law, AB 1572, phases out using drinking water on nonfunctional turf. For HOAs, common interest developments, and community service organizations, the deadline is January 1, 2029. Homes inside an HOA aren't covered, but the association's common areas are.

The State Water Resources Control Board defines functional turf as mowed grass in a recreational use area or community space, such as a sports field or a park area used for community events. Mowed grass anywhere else is nonfunctional, including grass in street rights-of-way, in parking lots, and behind fencing that keeps people out.

What the law does and doesn't require

  • It restricts only watering decorative grass with drinking water. It doesn't require removing the grass.
  • Trees and other perennial plantings that aren't grass can still be watered with drinking water.
  • Associations with more than 5,000 square feet of irrigated common area have to self-certify compliance, first by June 30, 2031, then every three years until 2040.
  • The State Water Board can postpone a deadline by up to three years for economic hardship, critical business need, or health and safety impacts.

How to get ready

Start by marking which lawn areas residents actually use and which are only for looks. For the decorative areas, the options are to stop watering them, switch to recycled water where it's available, or convert them to planting on drip irrigation. A conversion is installation work for a licensed landscape contractor, and many water providers offer turf replacement rebates, so check yours before you start. Your water provider can also tell you whether a specific area counts as nonfunctional.

Owners' yards

Can an HOA stop homeowners from going drought-tolerant?

No. Under Civil Code section 4735, a provision in the governing documents or the architectural or landscaping guidelines is void if it prohibits low water-using plants, as a group or as a replacement for existing turf, or prohibits artificial turf. The association can still adopt landscaping rules, as long as they don't have that effect.

The same section bars fines or assessments against an owner for cutting back or stopping watering during a drought emergency declared by the Governor or a local government. An owner who installed water-efficient landscaping during a declared emergency doesn't have to remove it after the emergency ends.

This is general information, not legal advice. Your association's attorney can tell you how it applies to your documents.

Noise and equipment

Leaf blowers and work hours

In the City of Los Angeles, Municipal Code Section 112.04 bans gas-powered blowers within 500 feet of a residence at any time. The ban applies to both the person using the blower and the person who hired them. The same section bars lawn mowers, backpack blowers, edgers, and other loud equipment between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. within 500 feet of a residence.

An HOA is a residential property, so inside the city, gas blowers are effectively off the table on site. Other cities in the Valley and Ventura County set their own rules, so check yours and put the allowed work hours in the contract.

Where it goes

Green waste and California's organic waste law

California's organic waste law, SB 1383, requires businesses and multifamily properties with five or more units to either use organics collection or self-haul their organic waste to an approved facility. CalRecycle lists landscape and pruning waste as organic waste.

A landscaper who hauls clippings away counts as a self-hauler. The City of Calabasas's guidance, for example, says self-haulers have to keep organic waste separate from trash, take it to an organics facility or composter, and keep delivery receipts or weight tickets for five years.

In practice: ask your landscaper where the green waste goes, and don't let bags of clippings end up in the community's trash bins.

Changing vendors

Switching landscapers without losing ground

  1. Check your current contract's notice period and end date before you announce anything.
  2. Get a copy of each controller's program, or photograph every station's settings, before the outgoing crew leaves.
  3. Find the valves and backflow devices, and note any zones that are already broken.
  4. Collect gate remotes, keys, and pool and clubhouse access from the old vendor.
  5. Walk the property with the new landscaper and agree on a punch list for the first month.
FAQ

Common questions

What does HOA landscape maintenance usually include?

Routine visits for mowing, edging, weeding, trimming, and cleaning up walkways, entries, and pool areas, plus irrigation checks and controller adjustments, seasonal work like leaf drop and storm cleanup, and hauling green waste. Larger tree work and installation projects are usually quoted separately.

How often should an HOA's landscaping be serviced?

It depends on the property and the season. Entries, walkways, and pool areas usually get the most frequent visits, slopes and back areas less often, and growth slows in winter. A walkthrough is the best way to set the schedule, and the contract should list frequency by area.

Does California's nonfunctional turf ban apply to HOAs?

Yes, to common areas. Starting January 1, 2029, HOA and common interest development common areas can't use drinking water on nonfunctional turf, meaning mowed grass that isn't in a recreational use area or community space. Homes inside the HOA aren't covered, trees and other plants can still be watered, and the law doesn't require removing grass.

Can an HOA ban artificial turf or drought-tolerant plants?

No. Civil Code section 4735 makes a rule void if it prohibits low water-using plants, as a group or as a replacement for existing turf, or prohibits artificial turf. The association can still set landscaping rules that don't have that effect.

Who is responsible for landscaping in an HOA, the association or the owner?

Your CC&Rs control. If they don't say otherwise, Civil Code section 4775 makes the association responsible for the common area and each owner responsible for maintaining the exclusive use common area attached to their unit, such as a patio, while the association handles repairing and replacing that area.

Are gas leaf blowers allowed at HOAs in Los Angeles?

Not in the City of Los Angeles. Municipal Code Section 112.04 bans gas-powered blowers within 500 feet of a residence at any time, and the ban applies to both the person using the blower and the person who hired them. Other cities set their own rules.

Does an HOA landscaper need a contractor's license?

For routine maintenance, generally no. The Contractors State License Board says gardeners who maintain landscaping and are paid on a regular basis generally don't need a C-27 license. A project needs a licensed contractor once it reaches $1,000 in labor and materials, needs a permit, or involves hired workers, and tree pruning above 15 feet needs a license too.
