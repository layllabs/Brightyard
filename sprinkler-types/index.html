Types of sprinkler systems, and what each one is best for

Most Valley yards run on a mix of spray heads, rotors, and drip, often put in years apart. Each type puts water down at a different speed, and that speed decides how long it can run before the water ends up in the gutter. Here's how the common types compare, where each one fits, and what to change when your system is fighting your soil.

By Joaquin, owner of Brightyard · Last reviewed November 16, 2026

The short version

  • Spray heads suit small lawns and beds, but they water fast. On flat clay, a spray zone can start running off in about 4 minutes.
  • Rotary nozzles screw onto the same spray heads and water more slowly and evenly. LADWP lets them run two 15-minute cycles instead of one 8-minute cycle.
  • Rotors are for larger lawns. They throw water 15 to 50 feet at a much lower rate than spray heads.
  • Drip is the best fit for beds, shrubs, trees, and slopes. The EPA says it uses 20 to 50 percent less water than conventional sprinklers.
  • Bubblers water individual trees and plants.
  • Never mix types on one valve. They put water down at different rates, so one area floods while another dries out.
  • The controller matters as much as the heads. A smart controller adjusts to the weather, and LADWP pays up to $200 toward one.

Small areas

Spray heads and rotary nozzles

Spray heads

Spray heads are the pop-ups most Valley yards have: a fixed fan of water in a set pattern. They're versatile and can water small and odd-shaped areas that rotors can't. The tradeoff is speed. A UC Davis and Department of Water Resources handout puts a typical 15-foot spray zone at about 1.6 inches an hour. The fine droplets can also drift and evaporate on hot or windy days.

Spray heads work best at about 30 psi, spaced so each head reaches the next one. Too much pressure turns the spray into mist. Pressure-regulating spray bodies fix that, and the EPA says WaterSense labeled ones can save a typical home as much as 5,600 gallons a year.

Rotary nozzles

Rotary nozzles, also called rotating nozzles, screw onto existing spray bodies in place of the standard nozzle. Instead of a fixed fan, they throw several rotating streams that put water down more slowly and evenly. The San Diego County Water Authority says they use about 20 percent less water than conventional spray heads.

For LADWP customers, they also come with a longer watering allowance: two cycles of up to 15 minutes per station on a watering day, compared with one cycle of up to 8 minutes for standard spray nozzles. On clay, that second cycle gives the soil time to soak up the first one.

Big lawns

Rotors

Rotors are sprinkler heads that turn slowly and throw a single stream or a few streams across a large area, typically 15 to 50 feet. Because they cover so much ground, each one puts down far less water per hour than a spray head. The UC Davis and DWR handout uses rates of 0.25 to 0.65 inches an hour for rotors, compared with about 1.6 for sprays. The lower flow cuts down on puddling and runoff.

The catch is space. A rotor needs room to reach its full throw, so it suits a wide lawn in Encino or Porter Ranch better than a narrow side yard or a small front lawn.

Beds, shrubs, and trees

Drip and bubblers

Drip

Drip irrigation runs low-volume tubing through the bed and lets water out slowly through emitters, right at the roots. The EPA says drip and other microirrigation systems use 20 to 50 percent less water than conventional sprinklers. UC Master Gardener materials put drip above 90 percent efficient, and a UC drip irrigation page puts sprinklers and hand watering at 50 to 70 percent.

Drip also keeps water off leaves, walls, and walkways, and it works on slopes where spray water would run straight downhill. What it needs:

  • Its own valve. The EPA says to put microirrigation on separate zones from spray heads.
  • A filter and a pressure regulator at the valve. The EPA notes that drip needs these extra parts to run efficiently.
  • Regular checks. Emitters clog and tubing gets pulled loose or chewed. Clean the filter as needed and walk the lines with the zone running a few times a year.

Bubblers

Bubblers flood a small area around a single tree or plant group. They put out more water than drip, so they work best inside a shallow basin that holds the water while it soaks in. For mature trees, water out toward the edge of the canopy rather than at the trunk. Our plant care guide has a starting schedule.

Why water ends up in the gutter

Clay soil and runoff

Much of the Valley sits on clay, and clay takes in water slowly. The UC Davis and DWR handout uses an intake rate of about a tenth of an inch per hour for flat clay. At spray-head rates, that means runoff can start in about 4 minutes. Rotors on the same soil can run roughly 9 to 24 minutes before runoff, depending on the nozzle.

The fix is to put water down no faster than the soil can take it:

  • Swap standard spray nozzles for rotary nozzles. Same heads, slower water, and under LADWP rules, two cycles instead of one.
  • Use the cycle-and-soak feature if your controller has one and your water provider's rules allow more than one cycle. It splits the run time into short cycles with a soak in between.
  • Convert beds to drip. Drip goes in slowly enough that runoff mostly stops being a problem.

Runoff isn't only wasteful. LADWP's rules prohibit watering that runs onto streets, driveways, and gutters, and they prohibit watering sidewalks and other hard surfaces.

The brain of the system

Controllers

A standard clock controller runs the same schedule until someone changes it, which often means a summer schedule running all winter. A weather-based controller, often called a smart controller, adjusts how long and how often each zone runs using local weather data. Some are add-on devices that work with an existing controller.

The EPA says a WaterSense labeled controller can save nearly 7,600 gallons a year. Soil moisture sensor systems are another option. They start and stop watering based on how wet the soil actually is.

Whatever you have, check it after a power outage. Our Santa Ana winds guide covers Edison power shutoffs, which can reset a controller's clock.

Local rules

Watering rules and rebates

If you're an LADWP customer
  • Sprinklers run on your assigned days: odd-numbered addresses on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, and even-numbered addresses on Sunday, Tuesday, and Thursday.
  • No outdoor watering from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m.
  • Standard spray nozzles: one cycle of up to 8 minutes per station per watering day.
  • Water-conserving nozzles: two cycles of up to 15 minutes per station per watering day.
  • No runoff, no watering hard surfaces, and leaks have to be fixed promptly.

LADWP rebates

  • Rotating nozzles: up to $6 per nozzle, with a 15-nozzle minimum and one rebate per household.
  • Weather-based controllers: up to $200 per controller for less than an acre of irrigated landscape. This can't be combined with the soil moisture sensor rebate.
  • Soil moisture sensor systems: up to $200 per unit.
  • Turf replacement: $5 per square foot, with pre-approval required before you start.

Calabasas, Hidden Hills, Agoura Hills, and Westlake Village

Las Virgenes Municipal Water District serves these cities. Its Irrigation Efficiency Retrofit Program covers up to $1,500 in irrigation upgrades for a qualifying household, and commercial properties may qualify for up to $3,000. The work can include converting spray zones to drip, rotating nozzles, pressure-regulating spray bodies, capping heads you don't need, and up to two weather-based controllers. Some zones don't qualify, such as hillside stations. It starts with a free Water Efficient Home Survey, run by WaterWise Consulting at (866) 648-2925.

Everywhere else

Many water providers in the Valley and Ventura County offer rebates through SoCal Water$mart. Amounts vary by provider and change with funding, so check your provider's current rebate before you buy anything.

If you manage commercial property or an HOA

A state law, AB 1572, phases out using drinking water to irrigate nonfunctional turf, meaning decorative grass that nobody uses. It starts with government properties on January 1, 2027, then commercial, industrial, and institutional properties on January 1, 2028, and HOA common areas on January 1, 2029. Single-family homes aren't covered. Owners don't have to remove the grass, only stop watering it with drinking water, so now is a good time to plan which areas switch to drip-irrigated planting.

Matching the system to the yard

What goes where

  • Small lawn: spray heads with rotary nozzles.
  • Large, open lawn: rotors.
  • Beds, hedges, and shrubs: drip.
  • Trees: drip rings or bubblers, out toward the edge of the canopy.
  • Slopes: drip, or rotary nozzles on short cycles.
  • Parkway strips and narrow side yards: drip. Spray in a narrow strip almost always hits the sidewalk or street.

Each of those should be its own zone, so each one can run on the schedule it needs.

Upkeep

Signs your system needs work

Run each zone for a minute or two on your watering day and walk it. Look for:

  • Geysers or bubbling from a broken head or a cracked pipe.
  • Mist or fog instead of droplets, a sign of high pressure.
  • Heads that are tilted, sunk, or blocked by plants that have grown in front of them.
  • Dry patches next to soggy ones, often from mixed head types or poor spacing.
  • Water pooling at the lowest head after the zone shuts off. Check valves on the heads can stop that drainage.
  • Water running onto pavement within the first few minutes.

Branches from a storm or a wind event break heads and knock drip lines loose, so walk the zones after either one. Our heavy rain guide also covers when to turn the system off.

FAQ

Common questions

What's the difference between spray heads and rotors?

Spray heads put out a fixed fan of water over a small area and water fast, about 1.6 inches an hour in a typical zone. Rotors turn slowly and throw water 15 to 50 feet, so they suit larger lawns and put water down at a much lower rate, about 0.25 to 0.65 inches an hour.

Are rotary nozzles worth it?

For most small lawns on clay, yes. They screw onto existing spray heads and water more slowly and evenly, and the San Diego County Water Authority says they use about 20 percent less water than conventional spray heads. LADWP allows them two 15-minute cycles and offers rebates of up to $6 per nozzle, with a 15-nozzle minimum.

Can I mix spray heads and rotors on the same zone?

No. A UC Davis and California Department of Water Resources handout calls it never acceptable, because they put water down at very different rates. One area floods while the other stays dry. Keep drip on its own zone too.

Is drip irrigation better than sprinklers?

For beds, shrubs, hedges, trees, and slopes, usually. The EPA says drip and other microirrigation use 20 to 50 percent less water than conventional sprinklers. Lawns still need sprinklers.

How long can I run my sprinklers in Los Angeles?

For LADWP customers, standard spray nozzles get one cycle of up to 8 minutes per station per watering day, and water-conserving nozzles get two cycles of up to 15 minutes. Watering days depend on your address, and there's no outdoor watering from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m.

Why does water run into the street when my sprinklers are on?

Usually because spray heads put water down faster than clay soil can take it in. On flat clay, a spray zone can start running off in about 4 minutes. Rotary nozzles, drip for beds, and fixing broken or misaimed heads all help. LADWP's rules prohibit runoff onto streets, driveways, and gutters.

Do smart irrigation controllers save water?

They can. A weather-based controller adjusts the schedule using local weather data, and the EPA says a WaterSense labeled controller can save nearly 7,600 gallons a year. LADWP offers a rebate of up to $200 per controller for less than an acre of landscape.

Are there rebates for upgrading my sprinklers?

LADWP offers up to $6 per rotating nozzle, up to $200 for a weather-based controller, and $5 per square foot for turf replacement with pre-approval. Las Virgenes Municipal Water District customers in Calabasas, Hidden Hills, Agoura Hills, and Westlake Village can get up to $1,500 in irrigation upgrades through its retrofit program. Many other providers offer rebates through SoCal Water$mart.
