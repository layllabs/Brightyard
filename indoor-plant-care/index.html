A houseplant guide for Valley homes

Indoor plant care: keeping houseplants healthy

Most houseplant trouble comes down to two things: not enough light and too much water. Here's how to get both right, what brown tips and little flies are telling you, which plants to keep away from pets, and how care changes in winter.

By Joaquin, owner of Brightyard · Last reviewed November 19, 2026

The short version

  • Light comes first. Most common houseplants do best in bright, indirect light. Long, stretched stems mean not enough light. Scorched spots after a move mean too much.
  • Water by the soil, not the calendar. Check the top inch with your finger. Water until some drains out the bottom, then empty the saucer.
  • Overwatering is the most common killer. When in doubt, wait a day.
  • In winter, plants slow down. Water less often, stop fertilizing until spring, and keep pots away from heater vents and cold drafts.
  • Little dark flies around the pot are usually fungus gnats, and they mean the soil is staying too wet.
  • Check before you bring a plant home if you have pets. True lilies can be lethal to cats, and every part of a sago palm is poisonous to cats and dogs.
  • Houseplants don't meaningfully clean indoor air. Keep them because you like them.

Start here

Light

Houseplants vary a lot. Some can take direct sun in a window, and others get by in a dim corner. Most of the common ones do best with as much bright, indirect light as you can give them. UC Master Gardeners warn that direct sun shining through a window onto a plant can burn its leaves.

How to read the signs

  • Too little light: long, stretched stems and leaf stalks. Yellowing, dropping leaves and no flowers can also trace back to light.
  • Too much light: sunburned spots, often right after the plant was moved to a brighter spot. Wilting and washed-out, yellow-green leaves can also come from too much light.

South- and west-facing windows get the strongest direct sun. East windows get gentler morning sun, and north windows are the dimmest. Daylight shifts through the year, so a plant that was fine in July may need to move closer to the window by December.

Plants for dim rooms

University of Illinois Extension names snake plant and ZZ plant as standouts for low light, along with pothos, philodendron, peace lily, Chinese evergreen, cast iron plant, and spider plant. Succulents want moderate to bright light and a fast-draining potting mix.

The big one

Watering

The curator of the UC Davis Botanical Conservatory, which cares for more than 3,000 plants, puts it plainly: overwatering is the number one killer of houseplants. Soil that stays wet smothers roots, invites rot, and draws pests.

When to water

  • Leafy plants: let the potting mix dry until it's barely moist.
  • Succulents and cacti: let the top inch feel mostly dry.
  • Check with your finger, about an inch down. You can also lift the pot. Dry mix is lighter in weight and color, and you'll learn the difference quickly.

How to water

  • Water thoroughly, with room-temperature water, until some runs out the drainage hole.
  • Empty the saucer. The water that drains out carries salts and minerals, and letting the pot sit in it pulls them back into the roots. Setting the pot on pebbles in the tray keeps it above the water.
  • Use pots with drainage holes. If you love a decorative pot without one, keep the plant in a plastic pot with a hole and set that inside the decorative one.
  • Don't overpot. A plant in a pot much bigger than its roots can't use all the water the extra soil holds, and it ends up acting overwatered.
Too much or too little?

Soft stems at the soil line, soggy soil, yellowing leaves, and soft or rotten roots point to too much water. Wilting and scorched leaf tips point to too little. But wilting can also come from rotting roots, so if a plant wilts in wet soil, slide it out of the pot and look. Healthy roots are white. Brown, soft roots mean rot.

White crust on the pot

Tap water and salt buildup

Tap water is usually fine for most houseplants. Softened water is not: the salt it carries can quickly kill plants, so use a tap that bypasses the softener.

Over time, fertilizer and water leave salts behind. You'll see them as a white crust on the rim or outside of clay pots, and they can cause brown leaf tips and root damage. The fix is to flush the pot every so often: take it to a sink or tub and run water through until it pours out the bottom. Scrub the crust off old pots before you reuse them.

The air around them

Humidity and temperature

Most indoor air is dry, especially in winter when the heat is on. Dry air causes brown leaf tips and favors spider mites. Cacti and succulents don't mind, but leafy plants do better with a little help:

  • Pebble trays: set pots on a tray of pebbles with water below the top of the pebbles.
  • Grouping: plants clustered together raise the humidity around each other.

UC Marin Master Gardeners say most houseplants do best at 70 to 80°F during the day and 65 to 70°F at night. Keep them away from heater vents and air conditioning, and out of drafts near doors and windows. Temperatures below 50°F can cause chilling injury, and sudden swings in temperature or light can make a plant drop leaves all at once.

Fertilizer

Fertilizing

Feed during the growing months, not year-round. UC Marin Master Gardeners say a monthly dose of diluted liquid fertilizer in summer keeps most houseplants healthy. In winter, lower light and cooler rooms slow growth, so skip it until spring.

More isn't better. Too much fertilizer causes brown leaf tips and root damage. On the other end, older leaves turning yellow-green can mean the plant needs feeding, especially nitrogen. Follow the label rate, or go weaker.

Moving up a size

Repotting

Signs a plant needs a bigger pot: roots coming out the drainage hole, a solid mass of roots around the root ball, or a plant that needs water constantly and wilts soon after.

  1. Time it for late winter or spring, when plants are about to start growing.
  2. Pick a pot only 1 to 2 inches wider than the current one, with a drainage hole. A pot that's much bigger holds more water than the roots can use, which leads to rot.
  3. Water the plant the day before.
  4. Use fresh, sterile potting mix, not garden soil.
  5. Set the plant so the soil line sits about an inch below the rim, firm the mix around it, and water well.

For a plant too big to repot, replace the top 2 or 3 inches of mix with fresh mix every other year or so.

Bugs

Pests

UC's pest management program says most houseplant problems come from care and conditions, like too little light or too much water, not from pests. To keep pests out, look new plants over before you buy, check them weekly for the first few weeks, and move any infested plant away from the others right away.

Washing the leaves with water now and then prevents many pest problems. Do it when the leaves can dry quickly.

Safety

Pets and kids

  • Lilies and cats: the ASPCA says true lilies and daylilies (Lilium and Hemerocallis) can be lethal to cats. Keep them, cut flowers included, out of any home with a cat.
  • Sago palm: every part is poisonous to cats and dogs, and the seeds are the most toxic.
  • Pothos and peace lily: both can cause mouth irritation and stomach upset. The ASPCA says the signs are unlikely to be severe.
  • Pet-safe examples: the ASPCA lists Boston fern, spider plant, and zebra haworthia as non-toxic.

Before you buy, look the plant up by name in the ASPCA's toxic and non-toxic plants list. If a pet eats something it shouldn't, call your vet or ASPCA Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply). If a child does, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.

This time of year

Winter care

  • Water less often. Growth slows with shorter days, so the soil stays wet longer. Keep checking the soil instead of running a summer schedule.
  • Stop fertilizing until spring.
  • Move plants closer to the light as the days get shorter.
  • Keep them away from heater vents, fireplaces, and drafty doors.
  • Add humidity with pebble trays or grouping, since heated air is dry.

Worth knowing

Do houseplants clean the air?

Not enough to matter. The idea traces back to a 1989 NASA experiment that tested plants in small sealed chambers. In 2019, Drexel University researchers reviewed a dozen studies spanning 30 years and found that the normal exchange of air in homes and offices clears indoor pollutants far faster than plants can remove them. Plants are good for a room in plenty of ways. Air purifying isn't one of them. Fresh air from open windows and your heating and cooling system does far more.

FAQ

Common questions

How often should I water indoor plants?

Water by the soil, not the calendar. Check the top inch with your finger: let leafy plants dry until the mix is barely moist, and let succulents dry until the top inch is mostly dry. Then water until some drains out the bottom and empty the saucer. In winter, the soil stays wet longer, so you'll water less often.

Why are the tips of my houseplant's leaves turning brown?

Common causes are dry indoor air, drafts, too much fertilizer or salt buildup in the soil, and watering problems in either direction. Flush the pot with water every so often, keep plants away from heater vents and drafts, and check your watering.

How do I get rid of fungus gnats?

Let the soil dry between waterings, empty standing water from saucers, and clear dead leaves off the soil. Yellow sticky traps catch the adults, and repeated drenches of Bti, a bacterial insecticide, control the larvae in the soil.

Which houseplants are toxic to cats?

The most dangerous are true lilies and daylilies, which the ASPCA says can be lethal to cats. Every part of a sago palm is poisonous to cats and dogs. Pothos and peace lily cause milder mouth and stomach irritation. Look plants up by name in the ASPCA's toxic and non-toxic plants list before you buy.

What houseplants do well in low light?

University of Illinois Extension names snake plant and ZZ plant as standouts, along with pothos, philodendron, peace lily, Chinese evergreen, cast iron plant, and spider plant. Even low-light plants need some light, so keep them in a room with a window.

Do houseplants clean the air?

Not enough to matter. A 2019 Drexel University review of a dozen studies found that the normal exchange of air in homes and offices clears indoor pollutants far faster than plants can remove them.

When should I repot a houseplant?

When roots come out of the drainage hole, fill the pot in a solid mass, or the plant needs water constantly and wilts soon after. Late winter and spring are the best times. Move up to a pot only 1 to 2 inches wider, with a drainage hole.

Should I fertilize houseplants in winter?

No. Lower light and cooler rooms slow growth in winter, so skip fertilizer until spring. During the growing months, a monthly dose of diluted liquid fertilizer keeps most houseplants healthy.
